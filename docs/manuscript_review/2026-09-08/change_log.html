We converted the original WLNM to generate links in food webs from observed data. The basic principle of WLNM is to learn the structural characteristics from a proportion of recorded (positive) and unrecorded (negative) links, whereby the latter is typically any absent links in the network. For a given link, a small subgraph formed around the vertices of the link is extracted and encoded into a fixed-size matrix according to the organisational roles of the nodes within it24. The encoded matrices from the training set are fed into a feed-forward neural network (i.e. a classifier), which uses the matrices to identify local topological features that constitute a positive, as well as a negative link. To perform link prediction between two given nodes, the classifier compares the features extrapolated from then encoded matrix of the substructure formed around them, and determines whether a positive or a negative link should exist22 (Fig. 1, Methods). To facilitate the application of WLNM to our ecological settings, we have made two major modifications. Firstly, food webs were represented as directed resource to consumer energy transfer networks, such that a candidate pair i⟶j denotes energy transfer from resource i to consumer j. The original WLNM was designed for undirected graphs only; therefore, we altered the encoding by retaining the full directed adjacency matrix, rather than collapsing the matrix into an undirected representation, allowing the direction of trophic energy transfer to be preserved (Supplementary Fig. 1). Secondly, the original WLNM uses a naïve strategy to generate negatives whereby randomly sampled unrecorded links are treated equivalently. However, an unobserved pair in a food web can range from an ecologically less plausible non-link to a plausible but unsampled interaction. To reduce this ambiguity, we implemented negative sampling based on trophic roles with hybrid top-up. Species were assigned to basal, intermediatetop-consumer, or top-consumerintermediate classes according to whether they acted only as energy sources/resources, only as energy receivers/consumers, or in both roles in the observed directed food web. Preferred negative examples were then sampled from directed non-links matching one of five trophic-role combinations: top consumer ⟶ top consumer, basal ⟶ basal, top consumer ⟶ basal, top consumer ⟶ intermediate, and intermediate ⟶ basal. If this restricted pool did not contain enough candidates to preserve the two negative per positive sampling ratio, all restricted candidates were retained and only the remaining deficit was completed using random valid directed non-links. With the expanded five constraint definition, 207 of the 290 food webs had a sufficient restricted pool, while 83 required the hybrid top-up procedure (Methods, Supplementary Fig. 2). As of the original study the size of the subgraphs is fixed at 10 nodes22 (Methods).

We compared food web properties between the empirical and pseudo food webs generated using 60% training data by referring to connectance, mean generality, mean vulnerability and mean trophic height32. For each empirical food web, 50 pseudo-web realisations were constructed by retaining the training positives and adding test candidate links classified as positive at threshold 0.5. The empirical reference consisted of all observed off-diagonal links. Connectance was L/[S(S-1)], where L is the number of directed links and S the number of taxa. Mean generality was the mean number of resources among taxa with at least one resource; mean vulnerability was the mean number of consumers among taxa with at least one consumer. Mean trophic height was calculated using the NetworkX-compatible prey-averaged trophic-level formulation on the largest weakly connected component, including basal taxa at trophic level 1. Singular, ill-conditioned or otherwise invalid trophic-level estimates were excluded.
